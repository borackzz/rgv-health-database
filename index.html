Start here: a young population carrying an old population's disease load

That one sentence is the atlas's thesis. Despite a median age in the low 30s, the Rio Grande Valley posts adult chronic-disease rates that rival much older U.S. populations, on top of some of the highest uninsured rates in the country. The gradient is consistent across all four counties and tends to be steepest in the poorest, most rural ones — Starr and Willacy — though these are also the two smallest counties, so their point estimates carry wide confidence intervals and rank differences are often not statistically distinguishable (read the CIs shown alongside each value). Because the population is young, age-adjusted rates run higher than crude rates for most conditions (see the ) — the age-specific risk is even more elevated than the headline numbers suggest.

What and where is the Rio Grande Valley?

The Rio Grande Valley (RGV) is the four-county region at the southern tip of Texas, where the Rio Grande meets the Gulf of Mexico and the United States meets Mexico. It is a majority Hispanic/Latino border region — one of the youngest and fastest-growing in the country, and among the poorest, with some of the nation's highest uninsured rates. Much of the population lives in , unincorporated settlements with limited infrastructure. This atlas covers all four counties: Cameron, Hidalgo, Starr, and Willacy.

Determinants & context

This is where the "why" lives. The numbers across this atlas describe a young population — median age in the low 30s — carrying the chronic-disease load of a much older one. That gap is not bad luck or personal failing. It is the predictable result of how people in the Rio Grande Valley live, work, and reach care: deep poverty, some of the nation's highest uninsured rates, widespread food insecurity, near-maximal social vulnerability, and the realities of a border region where much of the population lives in . The condition deep-dives point back here rather than repeating this story; this section is its single home.

The structural forces underneath the statistics

These are the reasons disease here is common, caught late, and hard to control — the causes that precede every outcome downstream.

≈500,000Texans live in colonias — about 14% of the RGV’s population

Colonias: growth without infrastructure

Roughly half a million people live in — unincorporated border settlements that grew from the 1950s onward, when developers sold cheap, often flood-prone farmland in small lots without water lines, sewer, drainage, or paved roads. In the Rio Grande Valley, colonias are home to about 14% of residents.

Infrastructure has lagged for decades. A 2015 Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas review found that fewer than half of Texas’s roughly 2,300 colonias had the full set of basic services; statewide, only about half of colonia residents have sewer service, and many rely on non-tap water. Unreliable water, distance from clinics, and substandard housing become real health costs — and they cluster in exactly the places where diabetes, uninsurance, and food insecurity run highest.

27–44%uninsured in the RGV, from all residents under 65 to working-age adults

Little coverage, few providers

The Valley has some of the highest uninsured rates in the United States. About 27% of Hidalgo County residents under 65 were uninsured in 2024 (Census SAHIE); among working-age adults (18–64) — the group shown on the map — the share is higher, roughly 36–44%. Because Texas has not expanded Medicaid, many adults fall into a coverage gap: earning too much for Medicaid but unable to afford a marketplace plan.

Coverage is only half the problem; supply is the other. The region is a federally designated medically underserved area () with one of the lowest physician-to-patient ratios in the state. The result is care that arrives late — diabetes and high blood pressure are often found only after they have already done damage.

22–28%of residents in each county are food insecure — vs 14% nationally (2023)

Poverty and the food environment

Deep poverty shapes daily health decisions. More than one in five Valley residents is food insecure, and the food that is cheapest and closest is usually the least healthy: fresh produce can be costly or far away, while calorie-dense packaged food is everywhere.

This is a central engine of the region’s obesity and diabetes. The Food Bank of the Rio Grande Valley distributes tens of millions of meals a year across the four counties — a measure of both the need and the community response. Food insecurity here is better understood as a feature of the local economy than as an individual failing.

≈49%of South Texas adults have bought medicine in Mexico

Care across the border

The border is part of how Valley families get care. With Mexico minutes away and insurance scarce, many residents cross for medication, dental work, and doctor visits. In one population-based South Texas study, about half of adults reported having bought medicine in Mexico, where the same drug can cost a fraction of the U.S. price.

This cross-border care is a real resource for families priced out of the U.S. system. It also splits prescriptions, records, and follow-up across two countries, complicating continuity of care — and it shows how far people will go to manage conditions they cannot afford to treat at home.

7.2%COPD in rural Starr & Willacy — highest in the region

Land, work, and weather

Geography and labor leave their own marks. Agricultural and outdoor work expose many residents to dust, pesticides, and extreme heat — part of why chronic lung disease runs highest in the rural counties, alongside their higher smoking rates. Long, hot summers and few safe places to walk or play discourage the physical activity that prevents diabetes and heart disease.

These exposures rarely show up in a single statistic, but they sit behind several of them — from COPD in Starr and Willacy to the region-wide rates of physical inactivity. The behaviors they shape are detailed one stage down, in .

Risk factors & behaviors

Between the structural and the diseases they produce sit the modifiable behaviors. In the Valley these are best read as products of the environment — long hot summers, demanding outdoor work, and few safe places to be active shape them far more than personal choice. Physical inactivity is widespread; smoking runs only modestly above the national level (13–17% vs 11%), which is itself part of the story: next to the region's large obesity and diabetes excess that gap is small — metabolic factors, more than tobacco, dominate the local risk profile. For a young, high-fertility border population this is the most conspicuous gap in the original atlas — and the numbers are pointed. Complications & mortality

Death is the ultimate outcome, and here it is measured directly — county-level, age-adjusted, and comparable to Texas and the United States — rather than inferred from prevalence. Each cause below is a real mappable layer (choose it under Outcome & vital layer in the sidebar). Two patterns stand out: premature death before age 75 runs above the national rate in three of four counties and is highest in rural Willacy and Starr (Hidalgo sits below it); and stroke death rates sit below the Texas rate in every county, with heart-disease deaths below the U.S. rate everywhere except Starr — a reminder that high prevalence and death do not always move together.

Injury, violence & substance-use mortality

The Valley's injury-death profile runs against the border stereotype. Suicide, homicide, firearm and overdose deaths all sit well below Texas and U.S. levels — part of the broader Hispanic-mortality advantage. System response

The burden is persistent — but because it is structural rather than inevitable, it is also changeable. This is the last stage of the chain: what the region is doing about everything upstream. After generations of underinvestment, the Valley is building its own health infrastructure and has pioneered some of public health's most effective community models — and a live research feed tracks the science as it appears.

1990sLower-RGV promotoras first organized across programs

Promotoras: the Valley's own answer

The Valley helped pioneer one of public health's most effective ideas: the , or community health worker — a trusted neighbor, usually bilingual and from the community, who connects families to care, explains conditions in plain language, and follows up where clinics cannot reach. Lower-RGV promotoras organized across programs as far back as the 1990s.

Today the model is formally trained and certified through institutions like South Texas College and UTRGV. Where trust, language, and distance are barriers, promotoras are often the most reliable bridge between the health system and the people it is meant to serve.

2016UTRGV welcomed the first class of its School of Medicine

A region building its own capacity

None of this is fixed in place. After generations of underinvestment, the Valley is building its own health infrastructure: UTRGV opened its School of Medicine in 2016, new NIH-funded research centers are targeting the region's cancers and diabetes, and community programs increasingly focus on prevention and the built environment.

Coverage and low-cost care exist but can be hard to find: community health centers () serve patients regardless of insurance or ability to pay. Knowing where to go is half the battle — and understanding the causes upstream is the first step toward changing them.

Glossary

Plain-language definitions of the terms a newcomer needs to read this atlas. Key terms link here from where they first appear.

Crude rate
The share of a population with a condition, counted directly, with no adjustment for age. Because the Rio Grande Valley is unusually young, crude rates understate its age-specific burden.
Age-adjusted rate
A rate mathematically re-weighted so populations with different age structures can be compared fairly — as if every area had the same age mix. Here, age-adjusted rates run higher than crude ones for most conditions, revealing that the Valley's youth is masking even greater age-specific risk.
2000 U.S. standard population
The fixed age distribution (the U.S. population in the year 2000) that CDC uses as the common yardstick when age-adjusting. Naming it makes the direction of the adjustment interpretable.
Prevalence
How many people currently have a condition (a snapshot). Most of this atlas's chronic-disease measures are prevalence.
Incidence
How many new cases arise over a period of time (a flow). The cancer registry figures are incidence — new diagnoses per year — not prevalence.
Model-based small-area estimate
A statistically modeled value for a small place (county, tract) built from survey data plus demographics, rather than a direct headcount. CDC PLACES estimates work this way; they are best read as reliable patterns, not exact counts.
Confidence interval (CI)
The range within which the true value most likely falls (usually 95%). When two counties' intervals overlap heavily — common for small Starr and Willacy — their difference may not be statistically real, even if the point estimates differ.
Social Vulnerability Index (SVI)
A CDC/ATSDR percentile (0–1) summarizing how vulnerable a community is to disaster and disease based on poverty, housing, transportation, and similar factors. All four RGV counties sit in the most vulnerable decile.
Colonia
An unincorporated border settlement, often built on cheap flood-prone land, that frequently lacks water, sewer, drainage, or paved roads. About 14% of RGV residents live in colonias.
Promotora
A community health worker — typically a trusted, bilingual neighbor from the community — who bridges families and the health system, explaining conditions and following up where clinics cannot reach.
FQHC (Federally Qualified Health Center)
A community health center that receives federal support to serve patients regardless of insurance or ability to pay, on a sliding fee scale. A key safety net in a region with some of the nation's highest uninsurance.
HPSA / MUA
Federal designations for places with too few providers: a Health Professional Shortage Area and a Medically Underserved Area. The RGV carries both, quantifying the "few providers" side of its access gap.
Age-specific risk
The risk of a condition within a given age group. Because the Valley is young, its overall (crude) rates can look modest even when the risk faced by people of a given age is markedly elevated — the core of the atlas's thesis.
Census tract
A small, relatively stable geographic unit (a few thousand people) used by the Census Bureau. Tract-level maps reveal neighborhood patterns that county averages hide.

Requires internet; tract estimates are crude only (age-adjustment is not published at tract level). If a layer can't load, the app falls back to the county view and all other panels keep working.

Chronic kidney disease is not published in the mappable PLACES measure set, so it is covered narratively (see Conditions) rather than mapped, to avoid fabricated county values.

Complications & cost: Cureus 2024 (Texas hospital-discharge data). Social drivers: Census SAIPE 2024 (poverty, all ages), ACS 2020–2024 5-year estimates (median household income), Feeding America Map the Meal Gap 2025 (food insecurity, 2023 data, via County Health Rankings & Roadmaps) and CDC/ATSDR SVI 2022 (the latest release). Texas & U.S. benchmark values come from the same PLACES release as the county data: the U.S. value is the PLACES national estimate, and the Texas value is the adult-population-weighted average of the Texas county estimates (computed for this atlas, because PLACES publishes no state row). Every "× U.S." comparison therefore uses the same model, survey year and measure definition.

Age adjustment: PLACES age-adjusts prevalence to the 2000 U.S. standard population (the CDC standard). The crude/age-adjusted toggle switches between the two. Because the Rio Grande Valley skews young, age-adjusting shifts weight toward older age groups, so age-adjusted rates run higher than crude rates for most conditions here — the age-specific risk is greater than the crude headline suggests.

The "44%" figure: the widely-cited Cureus diabetes prevalence (~44%) comes from hospitalized patients, not the general population, and is not comparable to the population-prevalence estimates mapped here. It appears in the diabetes narrative for context only.

Mortality, life expectancy, cancer & maternal-infant layers (updated September 2026)

Cause-specific mortality (county): heart-disease and stroke death rates are from the CDC Interactive Atlas of Heart Disease & Stroke (Division for Heart Disease and Stroke Prevention; data.cdc.gov mri7-5jtw and y5ii-knwc), a 3-year average for 2022–2024, age-standardized to the 2000 U.S. standard population, for adults 35+, spatially smoothed (a model-based estimate; the atlas datasets publish no county confidence interval). Premature death (all causes before age 75, age-adjusted) is from NCHS mortality files via County Health Rankings & Roadmaps (measure v127, 2021–2023, 2026 data update). County age-adjusted rates for diabetes, chronic lower-respiratory disease and kidney disease require a CDC WONDER underlying-cause-of-death interactive extract (no clean open API; data-use restrictions), so they are covered narratively rather than shown as invented values.

Life expectancy at birth (county): period life expectancy for 2021–2023 from NCHS mortality files via County Health Rankings & Roadmaps (measure v147, 2026 data update), with 95% confidence intervals. It is the current county series; the tract layer below is older because NCHS has not published newer small-area life tables.

Life expectancy at birth (census tract): a bundled static file (rgv_usaleep.js) from the NCHS USALEEP project — U.S. Life Expectancy at Birth by Census Tract, 2010–2015 (data.cdc.gov 5h56-n989) — filtered to the four counties' 2010 census tracts (219 tracts; 10 suppressed by NCHS and labelled as such). Each tract carries a period life expectancy and standard error; tooltips show a 95% CI (estimate ± 1.96·SE). The tract layer joins to Census 2010 TIGERweb geometry fetched live; if that geometry can't load, the county summaries still display.

Maternal & infant health (county): from NCHS Natality & Linked Birth/Infant Death files via County Health Rankings & Roadmaps (2026 data update). Windows are multi-year pooled to stabilize small-county rates: infant mortality 2017–2023 (Willacy suppressed for small counts), low birthweight 2018–2024, teen births 2018–2024. The atlas shows point estimates; reliability rests on the pooling window and CHR's suppression flags. Preterm birth, early/adequate prenatal care, and gestational diabetes are not cleanly county-available in these sources and are covered narratively — gestational diabetes in particular is not published at county level.

Cancer: the atlas's PLACES "ever told had cancer" prevalence masks site-specific excess. Registry figures here are county-level age-adjusted incidence per 100,000 (2000 U.S. standard, all stages) for 2019–2023 from State Cancer Profiles (NCI & CDC; Texas Cancer Registry; U.S. = SEER+NPCR), with 95% confidence intervals. Willacy's cervical-cancer rate is suppressed (3 or fewer cases a year) and labelled. The chart plots each county's rate divided by the U.S. rate on a log scale, so sites with very different base rates can share one axis. Earlier regional figures (South Texas Health Status Review, 2005–2009) are cited as historical context only.

Suppression rule applied throughout: where a source withholds a value for small counts or does not publish it at county level, the cell is labelled suppressed / not available and covered narratively — never interpolated or estimated.

Infectious, environmental, behavioral/injury & capacity layers (updated September 2026)

What is county-level now: the September 2026 refresh replaced older regional (2005–2010) figures with current county data wherever an official county series exists — TB (Texas DSHS, 2024), HIV prevalence and chlamydia (CDC AtlasPlus, 2022), suicide, overdose, motor-vehicle, firearm, homicide and all-injury deaths (NCHS via County Health Rankings, 2017–2023), and provider ratios (HRSA AHRF / CMS NPI). The injury measures are also mappable county layers. Where a county series still isn't available — environmental exposures, falls and alcohol deaths, substance-use prevalence, syphilis and gonorrhea — figures are regional or narrative and labelled, never invented or downscaled to a county.

Infectious disease (county): tuberculosis new cases per 100,000 by county for 2024 (2023 kept for comparison) from the Texas DSHS TB Program; the U.S. 2024 rate (3.1) is from CDC. HIV prevalence and chlamydia rates by county (2022) are from CDC NCHHSTP AtlasPlus via County Health Rankings & Roadmaps. Small-county rates (Starr, Willacy) are volatile and flagged. Hepatitis C (tied to the liver-cancer excess), vector-borne disease (locally-acquired dengue in Cameron 2013, local Zika 2016–17, sporadic West Nile — small, unstable counts) and the COVID-19 retrospective are narrative. COVID cumulative deaths are county-reported totals (Hidalgo ≈ 4,000+ by Sept 2022; Cameron ≈ 1,962 by Oct 2021) — not age-adjusted rates and not comparable to the mortality layer.

Environmental & occupational (regional/narrative): childhood elevated blood lead (% of children tested) and work-related pesticide-illness incidence from Texas DSHS, 2006–2010 (via the South Texas Health Status Review). Extreme heat (CDC Heat & Health Tracker / EPHT), air quality (EPA AQS / AirNow), drinking-water violations and colonia water/sewer access (EPA SDWIS / TCEQ / TWDB / TDHCA) and South Texas groundwater arsenic (USGS) are real but not cleanly county-rated in the fetchable sources, so they are described and labelled. Surveillance-reported pesticide figures materially undercount farmworker exposure. These two indicators are historical (2006–2010); no current county series is published in open sources.

Behavioral health, injury & substance use (county): suicide (age-adjusted, 2019–2023), drug overdose (2020–2022), motor-vehicle crash (2017–2023), firearm (2019–2023), homicide (2017–2023) and all-injury (2019–2023) death rates per 100,000 from NCHS death certificates via County Health Rankings & Roadmaps. All but suicide are crude rates; Willacy's overdose and homicide rates are suppressed. Nearly all sit well below Texas and the U.S. (the Hispanic-mortality advantage) — not evidence of low distress against near-national depression and thin behavioral-health supply. Motor-vehicle deaths are the exception in rural Starr and Willacy. Falls and alcohol-related mortality require a CDC WONDER county extract not run here; substance-use prevalence is SAMHSA NSDUH substate only.

Health-system capacity (designations + facilities): all four counties are federally designated Health Professional Shortage Areas (primary care, dental, mental health) and Medically Underserved Areas/Populations (HRSA Data Warehouse, updated continuously). Residents per primary-care physician and per dentist come from the HRSA Area Health Resources File (2024–25 release), and residents per mental-health provider from the CMS NPI registry (2024), both via County Health Rankings & Roadmaps. The facility marker layer maps real, named, publicly-listed hospitals and FQHC sites (HRSA Health Center Program; CMS; provider sites) at approximate campus/city coordinates, clearly labelled approximate — an illustrative capacity layer, not a survey-grade directory. Willacy County has no hospital. Dialysis centers are described, not mapped. The user-facing care-finder lives in the System response section.
